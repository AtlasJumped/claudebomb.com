A concept for a universal smart module

One small cube. Any device. Your assistant.

Mote snaps onto a microwave, an air fryer, a refrigerator, an AV receiver, or a car. The device tells it what it is and what it can do. Your AI assistant and life dashboard take it from there.

The problem

Every maker builds its own smart features

Today each appliance brand writes its own software, ships its own app, and picks its own connector. Most devices never get smart at all, and the ones that do rarely talk to each other. Your life dashboard sees almost none of it.

Many apps

One app per brand, each with its own account and its own quirks.

Many connectors

Every maker spends research and development money on a different solution.

A chicken and egg gap

Makers skip a standard slot because no standard module exists, and no module exists because makers skip the slot.

How it works

A chip in the device, a cube on the surface

A tiny standard chip

The maker builds in a small, cheap, standard chip near the surface of the device. We suggest an FPGA-like universal chip, plus a small metal plate. One design could serve a microwave, a car, and everything between.

The cube attaches

There are three ways to connect, so makers can choose what fits. A magnet holds the cube onto a metal plate over the chip. An adhesive pad holds it where a magnet will not work. Or a small plug links the two for a firm connection. Details are just below.

The device introduces itself

It sends a name, a model, a device number, and a list of what it can and cannot do.

Your assistant takes over

The cube relays that list to the AI assistant you prefer and to your life dashboard. You ask. It acts, within what the device says it can do.

The three ways to attach

Preferred

Magnet

The cube sticks to a metal plate over the chip. No ports, no wear, works on any size of device.

Option

Adhesive

A sticky pad holds the cube where a magnet will not work.

Option

Plug

A small male and female connector for devices that need a firm link.

What it is for

What would you do with it

Take an air fryer. Today you may have to search the web for the right settings, then stand by it. With a cube on it, this could change.

Tell it what you are cooking

Say to your AI assistant what you are making, and the air fryer sets itself to the right temperature and time.

Photograph the box

Take a photo of the food box. Computer vision reads it and the right settings are chosen.

Get told when it is done

Your phone tells you when the food is ready. A microwave meal that needs stirring after three minutes could send you a text message.

Switch things on from far away

Turn on a space heater or an air filter before you get home.

A home robot cooks for you

Long term, a home robot could cook you a meal at a set time while every device runs on its own.

An honest limit. Waking up to a hot meal needs more than this. Some food cannot sit overnight in an oven. Frozen food and yogurt are examples. A cube can run a device, but it cannot make unsafe food safe to leave out.

No more buttons

Skip the buttons and go straight to the software

The buttons and knobs on a microwave or air fryer only send signals to the device's software. A universal connector could talk to that software directly. Then you would not need to press the buttons. You would tell your AI assistant what you want.

What makers could drop

Buttons, knobs, a custom app, and their own smart features. The hardware could be made on purpose simpler.

The downside

If the cube fails, the device has no other control. A fallback is wise: a basic button, or a second path to the device.

This is a long-range idea. Early products would keep their buttons.

Two models

One cube per device, or one per room

Simplest

One to one

One cube sits on each device. Simple to explain and to build. A first product might start here.

Also possible

Room node

One cube within about 5 to 10 meters of several devices serves them all, such as one cube for a kitchen. Real range depends on the radio used and on walls and other obstacles. We have not tested it.

Cost

What it might cost a maker

We have not costed a cube. Here is one real data point.

A real part today

The Espressif ESP32-C6 MINI module already supports Wi-Fi 6, Bluetooth LE, Thread, Zigbee, and Matter. It sells for $2.50 each at retail (SparkFun listing).

Volume and simpler chips

Prices for manufacturers buying in volume are typically lower. We have no quote, so we give no number. The chip built into a device could also be simpler than a full module.

The five dollar cube

This is a target, not a quote. The cube also needs a case, a magnet, and power, which this part alone does not cover.

Why cost matters more for small devices

Suppose adding this costs fifty cents, a dollar, or five dollars. On an expensive device the extra cost is easy to justify. On a small, cheap device it is harder, so that market would be the hardest to win. We want to get this right.

What the maker gets back

We are asking makers to add something, but they receive a feature in return. They would not need to build smart features, an app, or even buttons.

How this differs from Matter

Matter, running over Thread and other radios, already lets devices from many brands work together. That is real, shipping work. This concept differs in one way: it offers one physical connector for devices that have no smart features of their own.

What it could connect

Anything of any size

Kitchen

Microwave, air fryer, refrigerator. Start a cook, check a door, get a reminder.

Entertainment

AV receiver and speakers. Set scenes by voice or schedule.

Cars

Health and maintenance information flows into your dashboard.

Everything else

Garage tools, heaters, fans, pumps. If it has the plate and the chip, it can join.

For manufacturers

An open spec, built to be copied

This only works if makers adopt it. So we would publish open plans for the chip layout, the connector, and the capability format. Anyone could build to the spec. We would make the cube and a minimal reference circuit board that makers can drop into their products.

Cheap to add

A small board and a metal plate near the surface.

No lock in

Open plans mean makers are not tied to one company.

Less work

Skip the custom app. Describe your device once.

Open questions

What we do not know yet

Security

A cube that controls a car or an oven must resist hacking. Pairing, permissions, and updates need real design.

Manufacturer adoption

Makers must choose to add the chip. That is the hardest part.

Existing standards

Matter and Thread already aim at part of this, with wide industry backing. Mote would need to build on them or work beside them, not ignore them. They are related prior work, and we may find they cover more than we expect.

Power

Where does the cube get power? A device may supply a little, or the cube may need a battery.

Certification

Radio, safety, and appliance rules vary by country and take time and money.

The five dollar target

Plausible for simple parts at scale. Unproven for us.
